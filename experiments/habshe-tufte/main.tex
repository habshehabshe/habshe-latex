Other times it is a displayed derivation:
$$
ax^2 + bx = -c
$$
Divide both sides by $a$:
$$
x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x = -\frac{c}{a}
$$
Add $\left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2$ to both sides to complete the square:
$$
x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x + \left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2}{4a^2} - \frac{c}{a}
$$
$$
\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}
$$
Taking the square root and solving for $x$, we arrive at the quadratic formula:
$$
x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}
$$

  \textbf{Theorem (Cauchy--Schwarz Inequality):} For all vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$ in an inner product space, the following inequality holds:
  $$
  |\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle|^2 \le \langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{u} \rangle \cdot \langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle
  $$
  
  \textit{Proof:} If $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$, the relation holds trivially as $0 \le 0$. Assume $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$. For any scalar $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$, the non-negativity of the inner product norm guarantees:
  $$
  0 \le \|\mathbf{u} - \lambda \mathbf{v}\|^2 = \langle \mathbf{u} - \lambda \mathbf{v}, \, \mathbf{u} - \lambda \mathbf{v} \rangle = \langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{u} \rangle - 2\lambda \langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle + \lambda^2 \langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle
  $$
  Since this quadratic polynomial in $\lambda$ is always non-negative, its discriminant $\Delta$ must be non-positive:
  $$
  \Delta = (-2\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle)^2 - 4(\langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle)(\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{u} \rangle) \le 0 \implies 4|\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle|^2 \le 4\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{u} \rangle \langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle
  $$
  Dividing by 4 completes the proof. $\blacksquare$
\end{layoutfullwidth}

You can also use declarative commands (\texttt{\string\setlayoutfullwidth} and \texttt{\string\setlayouttufte}) when a transition should persist indefinitely rather than inside a local environment block.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Base Case ($n=1$):} The left-hand side equals $1$, and the right-hand side gives $\frac{1(1+1)}{2} = 1$. The base case holds.
\item \textbf{Inductive Hypothesis:} Assume the proposition holds for $k \ge 1$:
  $$
  \sum_{i=1}^k i = \frac{k(k+1)}{2}
  $$
\item \textbf{Inductive Step:} We examine the sum for $k+1$:
  $$
  \sum_{i=1}^{k+1} i = \left(\sum_{i=1}^k i\right) + (k+1) = \frac{k(k+1)}{2} + (k+1) = (k+1)\left(\frac{k}{2} + 1\right) = \frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}
  $$
  By induction, the equality holds for all natural numbers $n \in \mathbb{N}$. $\blacksquare$
\end{enumerate}

\heading{Display Math Skips: Standard vs. Short}

TeX applies different vertical skips above equations depending on whether the preceding text line overlaps the equation box.

\subheading{1. Preceding Line Overlaps Equation (Standard \texttt{\string\abovedisplayskip})}
This is a intentionally long line of text designed to extend all the way across the column, guaranteeing that its rightmost edge overlaps the centered equation below. Consequently, TeX triggers the full \texttt{\string\abovedisplayskip} ($1.0L$ natural):
$$
\mathbf{K} \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{f}_{\text{ext}} - \mathbf{f}_{\text{int}}(\mathbf{u})
$$

\subheading{2. Preceding Line Ends Early (Short \texttt{\string\abovedisplayshortskip})}
Short line:
$$
\mathbf{M}\ddot{\mathbf{u}} + \mathbf{C}\dot{\mathbf{u}} + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}
$$
Because the preceding text ended early, TeX automatically applied \texttt{\string\abovedisplayshortskip} ($0.0L \text{ plus } 0.2L$), drawing the equation closer to its introducing label.
